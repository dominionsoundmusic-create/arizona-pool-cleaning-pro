My pool is green, how do I fix it? It is one of the most searched pool questions in the country, and the honest answer starts with understanding what changed. Green water is nearly always algae, tiny plant-like organisms that bloom when the chlorine in the water can no longer keep up.

This guide covers why that happens, why a Yuma summer speeds it up, the safe checks you can make yourself today, and the point where it is smarter to hire help. It does not give chemical doses. Handling pool chemicals carries real risk, and the right amount depends on test results, pool size and the product label.

Why pool water turns green

Chlorine is the guard that keeps algae from taking hold. CDC asks home pool owners to keep free chlorine at 1 part per million or more, and at 2 or more when the water contains cyanuric acid. Cyanuric acid, often called stabilizer, helps chlorine last longer in sunlight. When free chlorine falls below that, algae starts to multiply. Several things push it down:

  • SunlightUltraviolet light breaks chlorine apart. A Pool and Hot Tub Alliance fact sheet says that without stabilizer, bright sun can destroy most of the chlorine in a pool in about an hour.
  • HeatWarm water helps algae grow faster and uses up chlorine faster.
  • Stabilizer out of rangeToo little and the sun burns off the chlorine. Too much and the chlorine works slowly; CDC notes that stabilizers are known to slow disinfection.
  • Poor circulationA pump that stopped, a timer that reset, or a clogged basket leaves still water where algae can settle in.
  • A dirty filterA clogged or worn filter cannot catch the fine particles algae feeds on.
  • Debris and dustLeaves, pollen and windblown dust bring nutrients into the water and use up chlorine.

Why it happens so quickly in Yuma

In National Weather Service normals, Yuma logs about 118 days of 100 degrees or more each year, with the first one around late April and the last around early October. Visit Yuma reports that the city receives about 91 percent of its possible sunshine. Heat and strong sun work against chlorine every day of the summer, so a pool that nobody tends can lose its protection quickly.

Summer storms add a second push. The National Weather Service dates monsoon season from June 15 through September 30, and NOAA lists the Yuma area among places hit by blowing dust and dust storms. A pool that takes in a load of dust and leaves after a storm can turn cloudy, then green, within days.

Safe first steps you can take today

These steps do not involve mixing or measuring chemicals. They help you understand the problem, slow it down and give a pool company useful information.

  1. Keep people and pets out. Latch and lock the gate. Murky water hides anyone who slips under, and young children are most at risk: CDC says drowning kills more children ages 1 to 4 than any other cause.
  2. Test the water. With strips or a home test kit, read free chlorine, pH and, if your kit has it, stabilizer. Write the numbers down.
  3. Empty the baskets. Clear the skimmer basket and the pump basket so water can flow freely. Turn the pump off first.
  4. Make sure the pump is running. Check the timer or automation schedule and look for a tripped GFCI or breaker.
  5. Read the filter gauge. Compare the pressure to what it shows when the filter is clean. A much higher reading points to a clogged filter.
  6. Brush and keep it circulating. Brushing loosens algae from the walls so the filter and sanitizer can reach it.

Chemical safety. CDC warns never to mix pool chemicals with each other or with anything else, and never to mix chlorine products with acid, because toxic gas can form. Keep products in their original containers, dry, shaded and secured away from children and animals, and follow the label. Leave acid work to a professional.

Why is my pool green after cleaning?

It is frustrating to brush, vacuum and treat a pool, then watch it stay green. The usual reasons are simple. The algae was weakened but not killed, and it grew back. The chlorine was used up by the algae load before the job was done. The filter is too dirty to trap the dead algae, so it cycles back into the water. Or the stabilizer is so high that the chlorine works too slowly.

Angi also points out that metal parts in the system can oxidize and tint the water green. If the water looks clear but colored, ask a pool professional to test for metals rather than adding more chlorine.

What is the fastest way to clean a green pool?

Nothing turns a green pool blue overnight. Angi estimates 24 hours to five days to clear algae, depending on how severe it is and whether steps need repeating. A light tint with working equipment is at the short end. A dark pool with a clogged filter and a floor full of leaves is at the long end.

For a dark pool, the quickest safe path is usually a professional green pool cleanup. The company brings the testing, the filter cleaning and the repeat visits that a heavy bloom needs, and it carries the chemicals so you do not have to. The page on green pool cleanup in Yuma walks through that service and the published cost range.

Signs it is time to call a pool company

  • You cannot see the floor or the drain.
  • The water is still green after several days of your own effort.
  • The pump will not run, or a breaker keeps tripping.
  • You are out of town and cannot check on the pool yourself.
  • You are not comfortable handling pool chemicals.

After the water clears, steady weekly care helps stop a repeat. See the Yuma pool maintenance schedule or read about weekly pool service.

A green pool is a mosquito problem too

Yuma County Public Health says green, unmaintained pools are one of the major breeding sources for mosquitoes that carry West Nile virus. If you notice a pool in your neighborhood that has stayed green, report it through the Service Request feature on the Yuma County website or call Yuma County Vector Control at (928) 317-4584.

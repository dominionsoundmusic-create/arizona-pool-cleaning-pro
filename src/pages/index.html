Why a Yuma pool needs steady care

A backyard pool here deals with a mix of conditions that is hard on water and equipment. None of them is a reason to panic, but together they explain why so many owners keep a regular service schedule, and why a pool that is left alone for a few weeks in summer can change fast.

Long, very hot summers

National Weather Service figures for Yuma (1991 to 2020 normals) show that about 118 days each year reach 100 degrees or more, usually from late April into early October, and about 25 days at 110 or above. The normal July high is 106.7 degrees. Warm water lets algae grow quickly, and a pump that runs long hours in that heat wears on its seals and motor.

Strong sun all year

Visit Yuma reports that the sun shines during about 91 percent of daylight hours here. Sunlight breaks chlorine down, so outdoor pools usually carry cyanuric acid, a stabilizer that slows the sun's damage to chlorine. For water with a stabilizer in it, CDC guidance is free chlorine of 2 parts per million or more and a pH from 7.0 to 7.8.

Hard Colorado River water

The City of Yuma treats Colorado River water for its customers, and its own water FAQ calls that water hard, at 18 to 22 grains per gallon. Calcium hardness is the calcium content of the water, measured as a concentration. As pool water evaporates, its calcium stays in the pool, and each refill adds a fresh load of minerals. Over time that leaves the white crust on waterline tile and scale inside heaters and salt cells. Pool tile and calcium cleaning explains how that crust is removed.

Monsoon dust

Monsoon season, by the weather service's definition, runs from June 15 through September 30. A NOAA study of Arizona dust storms groups the low desert around Yuma into the part of the state with the most dust reports, and finds reports climbing sharply in July, when storm outflow can carry dust west toward the Colorado River. Dust and blown debris clog filters and use up chlorine, so the water can go cloudy within days.

Months away in summer

Many pool owners here are winter visitors. Yuma County says its population grows by about 90,000 in winter, when seasonal residents arrive. When those owners head north for the summer, their pools sit through the hottest months. That is often when water turns green. Yuma County's public health district calls green, unmaintained pools one of the major breeding sources of West Nile mosquitoes. If yours has already turned, read my pool is green: how do I fix it.

Check the company before work starts

The company you reach is independent, so choosing whether to hire it is your decision. A few questions make that easier. For regular service, ask what one visit includes, how often it comes, and whether chemicals are part of the price or billed separately. Before a repair, get the price on paper and write down the license number the company holds with the Arizona Registrar of Contractors (ROC). Liability insurance is not a condition of an ROC license, so ask the company to show you its coverage before work begins.

Pool equipment repair in Yuma covers the machines that keep the water clean and comfortable: the filter, the heater or heat pump, the salt chlorine generator, the timer or automation panel and the valves that steer the water between them. They sit outside on a concrete pad, in full sun, through every summer.

This page explains what each piece does, what usually goes wrong, how long published guides say each part lasts, and when a repair stops making sense. The pump has a page of its own: see pool pump repair for motors, seals and the federal variable-speed rule. For cracks, leaks and lights in the pool itself, see pool repair.

Yuma Pool Cleaning Pro does no repairs and sells no parts. When you call the free line, you are connected to an independent pool company in the Yuma area, and that company inspects the equipment, quotes and does any work.

What sits on the equipment pad

Filter

Traps dirt, dust and algae as water passes through. Home pools use one of three types: cartridge, sand or diatomaceous earth (DE), a fine powder that coats grids inside the tank.

Heater or heat pump

Gas models fire a burner, fed by natural gas or propane, and heat water quickly. A heat pump uses electricity to move warmth from the outdoor air into the water, the way an air conditioner works in reverse.

Salt chlorine generator

A control box and a cell in the plumbing. Low-salt water passes over charged plates in the cell, which turn some of the salt into chlorine.

Timer or automation

Decides when the pump, heater, lights and cleaner run. It may be a simple clock with pins or a digital panel with an app.

Valves

Diverter and check valves send water to the right place, such as pool or spa, or stop it flowing backward. Worn internal seals let water slip past.

Pump

Moves all the water through everything else. Pump problems are covered on the pool pump repair page.

Why Yuma is hard on pool equipment

Heat and sun. The equipment pad usually sits in the open. Yuma averages about 118 days a year at 100 degrees or higher, according to National Weather Service normals, so plastic housings, lids, gaskets and control boards spend months baking.

Dust. NOAA's dust storm research names the Yuma area and says dust storms peak in late July and early August, during the monsoon. Fine dust that blows into the pool ends up in the filter, and pressure can climb fast after a storm.

Hard water. City of Yuma water measures 18 to 22 grains per gallon of hardness, mostly calcium and magnesium. As pool water evaporates, those minerals stay and concentrate. They form scale inside heater heat exchangers and on salt cell plates, and both work worse when coated.

Filters: the pressure gauge tells you a lot

Most filters have a pressure gauge on top. It is one of the few things a homeowner can safely watch. Write down the reading right after the filter has been cleaned; that is your clean baseline. Angi's filter cleaning guide says a reading 8 to 10 psi above normal (normal is often 10 to 20 psi) means the filter needs cleaning.

Each filter type wears differently. Cartridge elements are rinsed and eventually replaced. Sand filters are backwashed, and the sand itself is changed every few years. DE filters are backwashed and recoated, and their grids can tear with age. A cracked tank, a leaking multiport valve or a broken clamp band is repair work. A tank under pressure can be dangerous to open, so leave anything beyond the gauge, the baskets and the manufacturer's own cleaning steps to a professional.

Heaters and heat pumps

When a gas heater will not fire, the cause can be simple or serious. Angi's troubleshooting guide lists a dirty filter, a closed valve or low water flow among the easy causes, since a heater may not fire when too little water moves through it. Harder causes include a failed ignition control, a bad gas valve, a dirty flame sensor and a heat exchanger clogged with scale. Angi notes that hard water can coat the heat exchanger and that removing that calcium usually takes a professional.

A heat pump is a different machine. The U.S. Department of Energy describes heat pump pool heaters as moving heat from the outside air into the water rather than making heat directly, and DOE material notes that heat pumps run most efficiently when the outside air is warm. Its fan, coil and refrigerant parts need a technician who works on that kind of system.

Summer is a good time to give the heater a break. APS, which supplies power in Yuma, Somerton and San Luis, lists turning off the pool heater in summer among its energy saving tips.

Gas smell near the heater? Leave the area at once and take everyone with you. Do not flip switches, start engines or use anything that could spark. From a safe place, call 911 and Southwest Gas at 877-860-6020, whether or not you are a Southwest Gas customer. Never try to find a gas leak yourself.

Salt chlorine generators and cells

A salt system saves you from handling chlorine jugs, but the cell does steady work. Angi's salt pool checklist suggests looking at the cell for mineral buildup every two months, and HomeGuide's salt pool guide says cells are often cleaned every three to four months. Warm weather and heavy use call for more frequent cleaning. With hard Yuma water, white scale on the plates is common, and a scaled cell makes less chlorine. Cleaning usually means a mild acid soak, so ask the pool company to handle it.

When a clean cell still will not keep chlorine up, it may be worn out. Published guides put cell life at about 3 to 7 years. Angi's saltwater articles put a replacement cell anywhere from a few hundred dollars to about $1,100, depending on the cell. A control board fault or a wiring problem is electrical work for a professional.

If the system gives up and you switch to liquid or tablet chlorine for a while, follow the CDC's basics: no mixing of one pool chemical with another, original labeled containers only, and storage that is dry, cool, out of the sun and secured from children and pets. Read and follow the label every time.

Timers, automation and APS hours

A timer that lost power, drifted or got reset can make the pool look like it has an equipment failure when nothing is broken. Check that the clock shows the right time and the run times look sensible. If the timer is fine and nothing turns on, the problem may be a relay, a board or the wiring, which is electrical work for a professional.

Schedules matter on Yuma power bills. On current APS time-of-use plans, on-peak hours are 4 to 7 p.m. on weekdays all year, and from November through April there is a super off-peak window from 10 a.m. to 3 p.m. on weekdays. APS suggests setting the pool pump to run off-peak. Since 2025, many new replacement pump motors must offer variable speed under a federal rule; the pump repair page explains it.

Safe checks before you call

  • Read the filter gauge and compare it with your clean baseline.
  • Empty the skimmer and pump baskets; poor flow keeps heaters and salt cells from running.
  • Look at the timer or automation schedule and the clock time.
  • Check the water level at the skimmer.
  • Look for drips, puddles or white crust at the equipment pad, and clear leaves or dust piled around it.
  • A tripped breaker or GFCI may be reset one time only; a second trip means leave the circuit off and get help.

Stop there. Do not open heater panels, gas parts, control boards or wiring. When you talk to a company, ask what failed and how they know, whether repairing or replacing makes more sense for a part of that age, and for the price of each option in writing. Gas lines and electrical work past the first disconnect fall outside the scope of Arizona's R-6 pool service classification, so a new gas line to a heater or a new circuit goes to a contractor licensed for that work.

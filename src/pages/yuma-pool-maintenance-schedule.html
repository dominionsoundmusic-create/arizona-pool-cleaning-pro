A pool maintenance schedule is just a list of small jobs done on time, so the water never gets far from clean and balanced. The national lists are a good start, but a Yuma pool lives through months of triple-digit heat, dust that blows in during the monsoon, very hard tap water, and a long winter when many owners are here, followed by a summer when many of them are not. This guide gives you the basic weekly, monthly and quarterly list, then a Yuma calendar that shows when each part of the year needs extra attention.

A note on who wrote this: Yuma Pool Cleaning Pro does no pool work itself. It runs a free number that puts callers through to an independently owned pool company in the Yuma area, and that company handles the visits, the pricing and the billing if you decide to hand the schedule over.

The basic pool maintenance schedule: weekly, monthly, every few months

The pool industry trade group, known as the Pool and Hot Tub Alliance (PHTA), publishes a homeowner checklist. Here it is in short form, with a note on why each task matters.

Every week

  • Test chlorine, pH and alkalinity with a test kit, then adjust.
  • Brush the walls and floor. Brushing lifts algae before it can take hold.
  • Vacuum the floor.
  • Empty the skimmer and pump baskets so water flows freely.

Every month

  • Clean the filter as needed: backwash a sand filter or rinse a cartridge, following the maker's directions.
  • Shock (oxidize) the water.
  • Look over the pump, filter and heater for leaks, noise or wear.

Every few months

  • Test total dissolved solids, the minerals and other material that build up in the water over time.
  • Clean the tile and grout at the waterline.
  • Keep a simple log of test results and work done.

Testing deserves its own line. The CDC page on home pool water asks owners to check chlorine and pH no less than two times each day, with more checks when the pool is crowded. Few households test that often, which is one reason a quick check between weekly visits is worth the minute it takes. The CDC targets for a home pool are:

  • pH7.0 to 7.8. Chlorine works more slowly as pH rises.
  • Free chlorineat least 1 part per million (ppm).
  • Free chlorine with stabilizerat least 2 ppm when cyanuric acid or a stabilized chlorine product is in use.

Leaving for the summer, coming back in the fall

A pool left alone through a Yuma summer can go from clear to green while its owner is far away. Two short checklists help.

Before you leave for the summer

  • Arrange weekly care with a pool service company or a reliable person, and leave gate access and a phone number.
  • Empty the skimmer and pump baskets and clean the filter as the maker directs.
  • Confirm the timer runs every day and avoids the APS on-peak hours.
  • Fill the water to about the middle of the skimmer opening.
  • Store chemicals in their original containers, dry, out of the sun, secured from children and pets, and within the label's temperature limit. A closed garage gets very hot in summer.
  • Check that the pool gate still closes and latches by itself.

When you come back in the fall

  • Look before anyone swims. If you cannot see the floor clearly, keep everyone out, especially children.
  • Empty the baskets and read the filter pressure gauge.
  • Check the timer, the water level and the equipment pad for drips or leaks.
  • Look for white crust along the tile line.
  • Test the water, or have the pool company test it, before the first swim.
  • If the water is green or cloudy, read my pool is green, how do I fix it.

Sun, evaporation and hard water: the Yuma part of pool water maintenance

Sun and stabilizer

Sunlight is one of the factors the CDC names for falling chlorine readings. Visit Yuma reports that the sun shines here about 91 percent of daylight hours. On the EPA and NWS UV index scale, a reading of 11 or more counts as extreme. Many pools use cyanuric acid, often called stabilizer or conditioner, which forms a weak bond with chlorine and shields it from the sun. The tradeoff is that chlorine kills germs more slowly with stabilizer present, which is why the CDC asks for at least 2 ppm instead of 1 when it is used. How much stabilizer a pool should carry is a question for the pool technician who tests it.

Evaporation and topping off

The EPA WaterSense program estimates that a typical uncovered pool can lose 12,000 to 31,000 gallons a year to evaporation, depending on where it is, and that a pool cover can prevent up to 95 percent of that loss. In dry desert heat, plan to check the water level at least weekly in summer. A level that sinks far down the skimmer opening lets the pump draw air. If the drop seems too fast, a bucket test (a bucket of pool water set on a step, with both levels marked) shows whether you are seeing evaporation or a possible leak.

Hard water and the tile line

City of Yuma water comes from the Colorado River, and the city's water FAQ calls it hard: 18 to 22 grains per gallon. Evaporation takes only water, leaving calcium and other minerals in the pool, and each top-off brings in a fresh dose. Month after month, the result is a pale, rough crust on the tile right at the water line. Brushing the tile on schedule slows it down. Heavy scale is a separate job; see pool tile cleaning and calcium scale.

Pump timers and APS hours

APS current time-of-use plans charge the most from 4 to 7 p.m. on weekdays, and APS advises running the pool pump off-peak. From November to April, APS plans add a super off-peak window from 10 a.m. to 3 p.m. on weekdays, and APS says running the pump then could save a customer up to $120 a year. Older APS plans use a 3 to 8 p.m. peak, so check your bill. In Wellton, electricity comes from the Wellton-Mohawk Irrigation and Drainage District, and in the Foothills or Gadsden your bill names your provider.

Pool maintenance tips and tricks that are safe for homeowners

  • Skim before you brush. Taking leaves and dust off the top first keeps them from sinking while you work.
  • Note the filter gauge when the filter is clean. Write that number down. Your filter maker's directions say how far above it the pressure can climb before cleaning.
  • Mark the water line on a calm day. A piece of tape at the skimmer makes fast drops easy to spot.
  • Check the timer after any power outage. Make sure the clock still shows the right time, so the pump runs at the hours you chose.
  • Keep the deck and equipment pad clear. Gravel, palm fronds and blown debris end up in the baskets.
  • Reset a tripped GFCI or breaker one time. If it shuts off again, leave the power off and phone a pro. Do not open the equipment or touch wiring.
  • Keep a log. Dates, test readings and anything odd you noticed help a pool technician find a pattern.

Chemical safety first. The CDC says never mix pool chemicals with each other or with anything else; chlorine and acid together can make toxic gas. Keep products in their original labeled containers, dry, cool, out of direct sun and secured away from children and pets, and follow the label. Leave acid work to a professional. If you ever smell gas near a pool heater, leave the area right away, do not use switches or anything that could spark, and from a safe distance call 911 and Southwest Gas at 877-860-6020.

Leak or evaporation? Yuma makes it hard to tell

Every pool loses water to the air, and Yuma's weather speeds that up. The National Weather Service counts about 118 days a year at or above 100 degrees in Yuma, and the dry air and steady sun pull water off the surface day after day. EPA WaterSense says a typical uncovered pool can lose 12,000 to 31,000 gallons a year to evaporation, depending on where it is, and that a cover can stop up to 95 percent of that loss.

So a falling water level is not proof of a leak. EPA WaterSense gives a rule of thumb: losing more than 2 inches a week, or more than 3 inches a week in hot, dry areas, suggests a leak. Higher water bills, wet soil near the pool and an auto fill that seems to run all the time are other clues.

The bucket test: a safe check you can do

  1. Pick a calm day with no rain in the forecast. Turn off the pump, any cleaner and the automatic fill, and keep everyone out of the pool.
  2. Fill a bucket with pool water to about an inch below the rim. Set it on the first step so most of the bucket sits under the pool's surface.
  3. Mark the water level inside the bucket and the pool level on the tile or skimmer.
  4. After 24 hours, measure both drops. If the pool fell more than the bucket, the pool is probably losing water somewhere besides the air.

The bucket test tells you whether there is a leak. It does not tell you where it is. Repeating it with the pump running can hint at the plumbing: Angi notes that a bigger drop with the pump on points toward the pipes or the equipment.

Where pool leaks usually hide

Angi's leak guides list the same few places again and again. The skimmer, where its throat joins the pool shell, is often the first suspect. Return fittings, the main drain, and the seals around lights and jets come next. Underground pipes can leak too, and wet or sunken soil between the pool and the equipment is a common sign. Cracks in the deck or tile can also mean that ground under the pool has moved.

Finding the exact spot is a job for professional leak detection. A technician may put a small amount of dye near a crack or fitting and watch whether the water pulls it in. Pressure testing seals off each pipe and checks whether it holds. Electronic listening gear can pick up water escaping under the deck. Guessing and patching the wrong spot wastes money, so the test should come before the repair.

Before anyone drains the pool: some repairs need an empty pool, and an empty pool in Yuma's summer sun can be damaged. Let the repair company decide. In the City of Yuma, call City of Yuma Utilities at (928) 373-4500 before draining, because city code needs the Director of Utilities' approval to drain a pool into the sewer. The city's stormwater page also says to use the sewer cleanout on your own property, never a public manhole, and never let pool water run into the street.

Cracks, worn plaster and the waterline tile

Plaster and pebble finishes wear out in time. Angi's resurfacing tips say poor water chemistry damages a finish, and that low pH and low calcium hardness can etch it, leaving the surface rough and blotchy. Yuma's tap water has the opposite problem: the City of Yuma calls its water hard, at 18 to 22 grains per gallon. Hard water and fast evaporation leave calcium behind, and that shows up as a white crust at the water line. The page on pool tile cleaning and calcium scale covers that buildup.

A small crack or a chipped spot can often be patched. A finish that is rough all over, stained through or flaking off may need resurfacing. HomeGuide's resurfacing guide lists new plaster at about $6 to $8 per square foot, and pebble finishes cost more. Full resurfacing is construction work in Arizona, not a minor repair, so it needs a contractor with a pool construction license.

Tile at the water line takes the most heat and the most change between wet and dry. Loose or cracked tile lets water get behind it, so it is worth fixing early.

What "underwater pool repair" means

People search for underwater pool repair because they do not want to drain the pool. That is reasonable, since draining costs water and time, and an empty shell can be harmed by heat. Some repairs really can be done with the pool full. Bob Vila's review of pool patch products notes that certain two-part epoxies and patches are made to cure under water, mostly for small cracks and chips on concrete or plaster. Sealing around a light niche, a return fitting or a skimmer can sometimes be done the same way.

The same review warns that "works underwater" on a label can mean the product survives water once it has cured, not that you can apply it in a full pool. Underwater patches are often short-term fixes, too. A crack that keeps growing, a shell that has shifted, or a finish that is failing over a large area needs the pool drained and a proper repair. A careful technician will tell you which kind of fix your pool needs and how long it should last.

Pool lights, drain covers and safety

An underwater light mixes water and electricity, so it is not a job for a homeowner. CPSC says faulty underwater lighting is one of its main electrical concerns, especially in older pools whose fixtures may have worn out or may not have ground-fault circuit interrupter (GFCI) protection. A GFCI cuts the power when current leaks where it should not. CPSC advises testing permanently installed GFCIs every month. If the light's GFCI trips, you may reset it once. If it trips again, leave it off, keep everyone out of the water and call for help.

Drain covers matter just as much. The Virginia Graeme Baker Pool and Spa Safety Act, signed in December 2007, requires approved drain covers on public pools. For home pools, CPSC says compliant covers are not required but are highly recommended, and that nobody should swim in a pool with a loose, broken or missing cover. Sun and pool chemicals wear plastic covers down, so ask about yours during any repair visit.

How a pool repair visit in Yuma usually starts

{{ img('img-01.jpg', 'Aerial view of a kidney-shaped backyard pool with agaves, red boulders and open desert beyond the wall', 1344, 768) }}

A good repair visit starts with a diagnosis, not a part. Expect the technician to ask how fast the water drops, when you first saw the problem and whether anything changed recently, such as new landscaping or a deck crack. They may run a leak test, check fittings and the skimmer, look at the light and drain cover, and look at the finish up close. Then they should explain what they found and what they suggest.

Questions worth asking

  • What is causing the problem, and how did you confirm it?
  • Can this be fixed with the pool full, or does it need to be drained?
  • Is this a lasting repair or a temporary one, and about how long should it hold?
  • What will the job cost in writing, and what could change that price?
  • What is your Arizona ROC license number, and which classification is it?
  • Will any part of the job, such as wiring or a gas line, go to another licensed contractor?

Which Arizona license covers which repair

The Arizona Registrar of Contractors uses different licenses for different pool work. The R-6 license, Residential Swimming Pool Service and Repair, covers servicing and minor repairs on home pools and their accessories. It does not include gas lines, electrical work beyond the first disconnect, or plumbing tied into the home's drinking water. It also does not allow replacing a whole plaster or pebble finish. Building and repairing pools at a larger scale, including resurfacing, falls under pool construction licenses such as B-5. Arizona's small-job exemption is limited (about $1,000 per project under A.R.S. 32-1121) and never applies when a building permit is needed.

Leak or evaporation? Yuma makes it hard to tell

Every pool loses water to the air, and Yuma's weather speeds that up: National Weather Service normals give Yuma roughly 118 days of 100 degree heat or more each year. EPA WaterSense says a typical uncovered pool can lose 12,000 to 31,000 gallons a year to evaporation, depending on where it is, and that a cover can stop up to 95 percent of that loss.

So a falling water level is not proof of a leak. EPA WaterSense gives a rule of thumb: losing more than 2 inches a week, or more than 3 inches a week in hot, dry areas, suggests a leak. Higher water bills, wet soil near the pool and an auto fill that seems to run all the time are other clues.

The bucket test: a safe check you can do

  1. Pick a calm day with no rain in the forecast. Turn off the pump, any cleaner and the automatic fill, and keep everyone out of the pool.
  2. Fill a bucket with pool water to about an inch below the rim. Set it on the first step so most of the bucket sits under the pool's surface.
  3. Draw a line at the water surface inside the bucket, and put a piece of tape on the tile at the pool's surface.
  4. After 24 hours, measure both drops. If the pool fell more than the bucket, the pool is probably losing water somewhere besides the air.

The bucket test tells you whether there is a leak. It does not tell you where it is. Repeating it with the pump running can hint at the plumbing: Angi notes that a bigger drop with the pump on points toward the pipes or the equipment.

Where pool leaks usually hide

Angi's leak guides list the same few places again and again. The skimmer, where its throat joins the pool shell, is often the first suspect. Return fittings, the main drain, and the seals around lights and jets come next. Underground pipes can leak too, and wet or sunken soil between the pool and the equipment is a common sign.

Finding the exact spot is a job for professional leak detection. A technician may put a small amount of dye near a crack or fitting and watch whether the water pulls it in. Pressure testing seals off each pipe and checks whether it holds. Angi's guides say buried plumbing leaks generally need a professional with leak detection equipment, because digging blind can damage the pipes.

Before anyone drains the pool: some repairs need an empty pool, but whether to drain is a call for the repair company. In the City of Yuma, call City of Yuma Utilities at (928) 373-4500 before draining, because city code needs the Director of Utilities' approval to drain a pool into the sewer. The city's stormwater page also says to use the sewer cleanout on your own property, never a public manhole, and never let pool water run into the street.

Cracks, worn plaster and the waterline tile

Plaster and pebble finishes wear out in time. Angi's resurfacing tips say poor water chemistry damages a finish, and that low pH and low calcium hardness can etch it rough. Yuma's fill water leans the other way: the City of Yuma rates it hard, at 18 to 22 grains per gallon, and as pool water evaporates the calcium stays behind as a white crust at the water line. The page on pool tile cleaning and calcium scale covers that buildup.

A small crack or a chipped spot can often be patched. A finish that is rough all over, stained through or flaking off may need resurfacing. HomeGuide's resurfacing guide lists new plaster at about $6 to $8 per square foot, and pebble finishes cost more. Full resurfacing is construction work in Arizona, not a minor repair, so it needs a contractor with a pool construction license.

What "underwater pool repair" means

Most people who search for underwater pool repair simply want to keep the water in the pool. Some fixes allow that. Bob Vila's review of pool patch products notes that certain two-part epoxies and patches are made to cure under water, mostly for small cracks and chips on concrete or plaster. Sealing around a light niche, a return fitting or a skimmer can sometimes be done the same way.

The same review warns that "works underwater" on a label can mean the product survives water once it has cured, not that you can apply it in a full pool. A crack that keeps growing, a shell that has shifted, or a finish failing over a large area needs a drained pool and a proper repair.

Pool lights, drain covers and safety

An underwater light mixes water and electricity, so it is not a job for a homeowner. CPSC says faulty underwater lighting is one of its main electrical concerns, especially in older pools whose fixtures may have worn out or may not have ground-fault circuit interrupter (GFCI) protection. A GFCI shuts off power when current strays. CPSC advises testing permanently installed GFCIs every month. A homeowner may push the reset button on a tripped GFCI one time. A second trip means the fault is real: leave the circuit off, clear the pool and get a professional.

Drain covers matter just as much. The Virginia Graeme Baker Pool and Spa Safety Act, signed in December 2007, requires approved drain covers on public pools. For home pools, CPSC says compliant covers are not required but are highly recommended, and that nobody should swim in a pool with a loose, broken or missing cover. Ask the technician to look at yours during any repair visit.

How a pool repair visit in Yuma usually starts

Expect the first visit to be about finding the cause, before any part is ordered. The technician will likely ask how fast the water drops and when the problem began, then test, look over the fittings, skimmer, light and drain cover, and explain what they found.

Questions worth asking

  • What is causing the problem, and how did you confirm it?
  • Can this be fixed with the pool full, or does it need to be drained?
  • What will the job cost in writing, and what could change that price?
  • Which ROC license do you hold, and may I have the number to look up?
  • Will any part of the job, such as wiring or a gas line, go to another licensed contractor?

Which Arizona license covers which repair

Arizona splits pool work among several Registrar of Contractors classifications. R-6 (Residential Swimming Pool Service and Repair) is the one for routine service and smaller fixes to a home pool and its accessories. Gas piping, wiring past the first electrical disconnect, drinking water plumbing connections and a complete new plaster or pebble interior are carved out of R-6; construction classifications such as B-5 cover pool building and the bigger structural jobs. Arizona's small-job exemption is limited (about $1,000 per project under A.R.S. 32-1121) and never applies when a building permit is needed.

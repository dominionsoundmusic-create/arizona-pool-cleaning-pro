# Yuma climate and seasons: verified fact bank

- Date: 2026-10-21
- Method: verified via search summary (WebSearch, standard mode, restricted to official or credible domains). WebFetch was tried once on weather.gov and failed (DNS blocked), so no page was opened directly. Each fact below is recorded only when the number or wording appeared in the search summary of the named page.
- Searches used: 25 (plus 1 failed WebFetch)
- Scope: Yuma and Yuma County figures only. No comparisons with other cities are recorded for site use.
- Station: NWS figures are for Yuma International Airport (NWS station ID NYL), reported by the NWS Phoenix office (weather.gov/psr), which covers Yuma.

## 1. Heat (NWS Phoenix office)

1. Yuma averages **118 days a year at or above 100 F** (1991 to 2020 normal). Over the full period of record the average is 109 days.
   Source: NWS Phoenix, Historical Extreme Temperatures in Phoenix, Yuma, and El Centro, https://www.weather.gov/psr/ExtremeTemps (verified via search summary)
2. The 1991 to 2020 average **first 100 F day is April 25** and the average **last 100 F day is October 6**.
   Source: NWS Phoenix, Historical Extreme Temperatures in Phoenix, Yuma, and El Centro, https://www.weather.gov/psr/ExtremeTemps (verified via search summary; also listed on https://www.weather.gov/psr/FirstandLastTemperatures)
3. Yuma averages **25 days a year at or above 110 F** (1991 to 2020 normal); full period-of-record average is 20 days. Average first 110 F day June 11, average last August 26 (1991 to 2020).
   Source: NWS Phoenix, Historical Extreme Temperatures in Phoenix, Yuma, and El Centro, https://www.weather.gov/psr/ExtremeTemps (verified via search summary; the table was garbled in the snippet and columns were matched by city order, so use "about 25 days" wording)
4. **Record high: 124 F on July 28, 1995.** Yuma records began in 1878.
   Source: NWS Phoenix, July 2026 Climate Summary (PDF), https://www.weather.gov/media/psr/Climate/July2026Climate.pdf, and NWS Phoenix 2022 Climate Year in Review, https://www.weather.gov/psr/yearinreview2022 (verified via search summary)
5. **July normal average high: 106.7 F** (1991 to 2020).
   Source: NWS Phoenix, July 2026 Climate Summary (PDF), https://www.weather.gov/media/psr/Climate/July2026Climate.pdf (normal 106.7 listed against July 2026 average max of 108.4), and NWS Phoenix, Phoenix, Yuma, & El Centro July 2021 Climate Data, https://www.weather.gov/psr/July2021ClimateData (verified via search summary)
6. Annual normals (1991 to 2020): average daily high **88.1 F**, average daily low **63.8 F**.
   Source: NWS Phoenix, 2021 Climate Year in Review, https://www.weather.gov/psr/yearinreview2021, and 2022 Climate Year in Review, https://www.weather.gov/psr/yearinreview2022 (verified via search summary)
7. Recent observed years (not normals): 107 days at or above 100 F in 2021, 114 in 2022, 103 in 2023; 26 days at or above 110 F in 2021 and 20 in 2022.
   Source: NWS Phoenix, 2021 / 2022 / 2023 Climate Year in Review pages, https://www.weather.gov/psr/yearinreview2021, https://www.weather.gov/psr/yearinreview2022, https://www.weather.gov/psr/yearinreview2023 (verified via search summary)

### Checking the old website's claims
- "175 days above 100 degrees": **FALSE.** The official 1991 to 2020 normal is 118 days at or above 100 F (fact 1). The "175" number appears in secondary sources as a count of sunny days, not 100-degree days. Do not use 175.
- "Hottest city in Arizona": **NOT SUPPORTED.** Visit Yuma's own climate material notes that despite its sunshine Yuma is not actually the hottest city (Source: Visit Yuma, Climate, https://www.visityuma.com/about-yuma/climate/, verified via search summary). Drop the claim. Safe wording: "Yuma averages about 118 days a year at 100 F or hotter (NWS, 1991 to 2020 normals)."

## 2. Rainfall

8. **Annual precipitation normal: 3.28 inches** (1991 to 2020); the earlier 1981 to 2010 normal was 3.56 inches.
   Source: NWS Phoenix, 2022 and 2023 Climate Year in Review (Yuma TOTALS normal 3.28), https://www.weather.gov/psr/yearinreview2022, https://www.weather.gov/psr/yearinreview2023; 1981 to 2010 value from Phoenix, Yuma, & El Centro 2020 Climate Year in Review, https://www.weather.gov/psr/YearinReview2020 (verified via search summary). Safe site wording: "about 3.3 inches of rain in a normal year."
9. **January precipitation normal: 0.39 inches** (1991 to 2020, Yuma Int'l Airport).
   Source: NWS Phoenix, January climate summaries for NYL, e.g. https://www.weather.gov/media/psr/Climate/January2025Climate.pdf (verified via search summary)

## 3. Sunshine

10. Visit Yuma (Yuma Visitors Bureau) describes Yuma as the **"Sunniest City on Earth," with sun at 91 percent of daylight hours per year (a mean of 4,055 hours of a possible 4,456)**, and attributes the title to Guinness World Records ("the Guinness World Record recognizing Yuma as the sunniest place on earth").
    Source: Visit Yuma, Climate, https://www.visityuma.com/about-yuma/climate/, and Visit Yuma press release, Yuma's bold promise: Free food if sun doesn't shine, https://www.visityuma.com/media/yuma-s-bold-promise--free-food-if-sun-doesn-t-shine (verified via search summary). Note: the Guinness page itself was not seen; attribute as "Visit Yuma reports..." or "recognized by Guinness World Records as the sunniest place on Earth, according to Visit Yuma."
11. Yuma County's county profile puts it at about **4,050 sunny hours, a little more than 90 percent of the time**.
    Source: Yuma County, County Profile (PDF), https://www.yumacountyaz.gov/home/showdocument?id=16305 (verified via search summary)
12. City of Yuma says the city gets **more than 310 days of sunshine each year**.
    Source: City of Yuma, Living In Yuma, https://www.yumaaz.gov/business/economic-development/living-in-yuma (verified via search summary). (Sunny-day counts conflict across sources: 310, 350, 175. Prefer the hours/percent figure.)

## 4. Evaporation and pool covers

13. Reference evapotranspiration (a standard measure of how much water the air pulls from a well-watered surface) at the AZMET Yuma Mesa station averaged **80.65 inches a year** (1987 to 2002), with single years ranging about 77 to 85 inches.
    Source: University of Arizona AZMET, Yuma Mesa ETo/rain table, https://cales.arizona.edu/azmet/data/03etrain.htm (verified via search summary)
14. In 2026, the AZMET Yuma stations had already logged about **72 to 74 inches** of reference evapotranspiration year-to-date by October 14 (Yuma N.Gila 73.13, Yuma South 73.22, Yuma Valley 74.43, Yuma Valley ETo 72.41).
    Source: University of Arizona AZMET, Network-wide Summary for Evapotranspiration, https://azmet.arizona.edu/application-areas/evapotranspiration/network-wide-summary (verified via search summary). Note: AZMET ETo is for well-watered grass, not open pool water; use it only as "the desert air pulls more than 70 inches of water a year" type context, not as a pool evaporation rate.
15. A USGS report on western evaporation gives open-water evaporation rates reaching **more than 80 inches a year along parts of the lower Colorado River**.
    Source: USGS Professional Paper 272-D, Evaporation From The 17 Western States (Meyers), https://pubs.usgs.gov/pp/0272d/report.pdf (verified via search summary; older, regional figure)
16. EPA WaterSense: **a typical uncovered pool can lose 12,000 to 31,000 gallons of water a year to evaporation, depending on location, and a pool cover can prevent up to 95 percent of that evaporation.**
    Source: U.S. EPA WaterSense, Pool Covers, https://19january2021snapshot.epa.gov/watersense/pool-covers_.html (archived EPA page) and EPA WaterSense, Pool Water Efficiency, https://www.epa.gov/watersense/pool-water-efficiency (verified via search summary)
17. EPA guidance by cover type: solid, mesh or hybrid covers up to 95 percent; solar or bubble covers 50 to 95 percent depending on fit and coverage; liquid evaporation suppressants about 15 percent.
    Source: U.S. EPA, WaterSense at Work Section 5.3 Commercial Pool and Spa Equipment (PDF), https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2023-11/ws-commercial-bmp-watersenseatwork_section5.3_commercialpoolsandspas.pdf (verified via search summary)
18. U.S. DOE Better Buildings: **evaporation is by far the largest source of energy loss for pools; covers can save 50 to 70 percent of pool heating costs.**
    Source: U.S. Department of Energy, Better Buildings Solution Center, Pool Efficiency (PDF), https://betterbuildingssolutioncenter.energy.gov/sites/default/files/attachments/Pool%20efficiency_.pdf (verified via search summary). Note: the "95 percent" figure is EPA's, not DOE's.
19. Arizona Department of Water Resources pools guidance: **draining a pool roughly doubles the water loss** (versus evaporation), and recommends limiting pump run time to save energy.
    Source: ADWR, Pools and Spas (PDF), https://www.azwater.gov/sites/default/files/media/Pool&Spas_2015_1.pdf (verified via search summary)

## 5. UV, chlorine and stabilizer

20. CDC home pool targets: **pH 7.0 to 7.8 and free chlorine at least 1 ppm** in pools. For pools using **cyanuric acid** (stabilizer) or dichlor/trichlor products: **pH 7.0 to 7.8 and chlorine at least 2 ppm**. CDC lists **sunlight** among the factors that reduce chlorine levels, says disinfection takes longer with cyanuric acid present, and advises testing chlorine and pH at least twice a day (more often with many swimmers).
    Source: CDC, Home Pool and Hot Tub Water Treatment and Testing, https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-swimming/about/home-pool-and-hot-tub-water-treatment-and-testing.html (verified via search summary)
21. Cyanuric acid forms weak bonds with free chlorine, stabilizing the chlorine level in the presence of UV light, but it also decreases how quickly chlorine disinfects.
    Source: CDC Stacks, Effect of Cyanuric Acid on the Inactivation of Cryptosporidium, https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/151609/cdc_151609_DS1.pdf (verified via search summary)
22. A Michigan State University pool training manual describes cyanuric acid as acting like a "blanket" that surrounds chlorine and protects it from the sun.
    Source: Michigan State University, Swimming Pool Pest Management: A Training Manual for Commercial Pesticide Applicators, https://www.canr.msu.edu/ipm/uploads/files/TrainingManuals_SwimmingPool/SwimmingPool_WholeManual.pdf (verified via search summary)
23. UV index scale: **11 or higher is "extreme"**; NWS advises avoiding midday sun and using SPF 30+ sunscreen reapplied every two hours.
    Source: NWS, Ultraviolet (UV) Index Forecasts, https://www.weather.gov/ilx/uv-index; EPA, A Guide to the UV Index (PDF), https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/documents/uviguide.pdf (verified via search summary). No Yuma-specific UV number was found (see could-not-verify).

## 6. Monsoon, dust storms and wind

24. NWS defines the **monsoon season as June 15 through September 30**, the period when monsoon hazards are most likely.
    Source: NWS, The Monsoon June 15 - September 30 (PDF), https://www.weather.gov/media/twc/Monsoon.pdf; NWS Phoenix, 2025 Monsoon Season Review, https://www.weather.gov/psr/2025MonsoonSeasonReview (verified via search summary)
25. NOAA Technical Memorandum NWS-WR 290 says dust walls form from strong near-surface winds that pick up dust across **southwestern Arizona (for example, Yuma)**, and that **dust storms peak in late July and early August**, with the peak in monsoon rainfall.
    Source: NOAA Technical Memorandum NWS-WR 290, Blowing Dust and Dust Storms, https://www.weather.gov/media/wrh/online_publications/TMs/TM-290.pdf (verified via search summary)
26. The same memo notes that thunderstorm outflow (a cold pool of air) behaves as a density current, and that merged outflows can advance west and southwest to reach the Colorado River in western Arizona late in the evening.
    Source: NOAA Technical Memorandum NWS-WR 290, https://www.weather.gov/media/wrh/online_publications/TMs/TM-290.pdf (verified via search summary)
27. Dust storm driving safety ("Pull Aside, Stay Alive", ADOT and NWS): do not drive into a dust storm; exit the highway if possible or pull completely off the pavement; turn off all lights including flashers; set the parking brake and take your foot off the brake; stay buckled in and wait for the dust to pass.
    Source: ADOT, Drivers: Do you know what to do when confronted with a dust storm?, https://azdot.gov/news/drivers-do-you-know-what-do-when-confronted-dust-storm; NWS/ADOT, Pull Aside Stay Alive (PDF), https://www.weather.gov/media/twc/DustSafetyADOT2014Bold.pdf (verified via search summary)
28. NWS Phoenix event summary, August 25, 2025: severe storms moved east through Yuma County with damaging winds, and radar estimated 2 to 3 inches of rain in 3 hours in some spots.
    Source: NWS Phoenix, Dust Storm and Flash Flooding - August 25, 2025, https://www.weather.gov/psr/Aug252025 (verified via search summary; summary also named areas outside Arizona, do not reuse those parts)

## 7. Winter visitors (snowbirds)

29. The 2017 to 2018 Yuma Winter Visitors study estimated **71,000 winter visitors (about 35,500 travel parties)** who stayed at least 30 days in Yuma County during a **December to May** timeframe. Visitors staying less than 30 days were counted separately as "winter tourists."
    Source: Visit Yuma, 2017-2018 Yuma Winter Visitors Intercept Study, https://www.visityuma.com/about-us/2017-2018-yuma-winter-visitors-intercept-study/; Arizona Office of Tourism, Yuma Winter Visitor Study 2017-2018 (PDF), https://tourism.az.gov/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/Yuma-Winter-Visitor-Study-2017-2018.pdf (verified via search summary). This is the most recent count found; say "a 2017 to 2018 study estimated about 71,000."
30. Yuma County's website says the county's **population increases by about 90,000** due to sun-seeking winter visitors known as "Snowbirds" (no study or year given).
    Source: Yuma County, About the County, https://www.yumacountyaz.gov/visitors/about-the-county (verified via search summary)

## 8. Mild winters and frost

31. Freezing is very rare: the 1991 to 2020 normal for days with a low at or below 32 F is **0.1 days a year**, and 2023 had none.
    Source: NWS Phoenix, 2023 Climate Year in Review, https://www.weather.gov/psr/yearinreview2023 (verified via search summary)
32. Observed (not normal) January averages: January 2025 average high 70.1 F, average low 39.3 F; January 2024 average high 68.3 F.
    Source: NWS Phoenix, 2025 Weather Review, https://www.weather.gov/psr/yearinreview2025; 2024 Climate Year in Review, https://www.weather.gov/psr/yearinreview2024 (verified via search summary). Use only as single-year observations.
33. University of Arizona extension describes low desert winters as mild, with average highs in the mid-60s and lows in the 40s (general low-desert statement, not Yuma-specific).
    Source: University of Arizona Cooperative Extension, AZ1002 (PDF), https://extension.arizona.edu/sites/default/files/2024-08/az1002.pdf (verified via search summary; page title not shown in summary, so cite cautiously or omit)

## 9. Heat safety

34. NWS Phoenix issues **Extreme Heat Warnings** for the Yuma area; warning text advises taking extra precautions when working or spending time outside and rescheduling strenuous activity to early morning or evening, and drinking plenty of water. Warnings were issued for the Yuma area as late as October 6, 2026 (highs forecast about 103 to 107 F).
    Source: NWS, WWA Summary for Extreme Heat Warning Issued by PSR, https://forecast.weather.gov/wwamap/wwatxtget.php?cwa=PSR&wwa=extreme+heat+warning (verified via search summary)
35. Yuma County Emergency Management keeps a standing Extreme Heat Weather Alert page with cooling center and water site information.
    Source: Yuma County, Extreme Heat Weather Alert, https://www.yumacountyaz.gov/government/emergency-management/extreme-heat-weather-alert (verified via search summary)

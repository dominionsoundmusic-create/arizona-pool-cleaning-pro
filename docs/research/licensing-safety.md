## 1. Arizona Registrar of Contractors (ROC)

1. **R-6, Residential Swimming Pool Service and Repair.** Lets the licensee service and make minor repairs to residential pools and their accessories. Excludes plumbing connections to a potable water system, gas lines, gas chlorine systems, and electrical work beyond the first disconnect. Does not allow complete replacement of plaster or pebble pool interiors and decks.
   Source: ROC, License Classifications, https://roc.az.gov/license-classifications (verified via search summary, 2026-10-21)
2. **C-6, Swimming Pool Service and Repair (commercial).** Replace and repair commercial pools and accessories; plumbing connections to a potable water system, gas lines, gas chlorine systems and electrical work beyond the first disconnect must be subcontracted to a properly licensed contractor; no complete replacement of plaster or pebble interiors and decks. **CR-6** is the dual license combining the C-6 and R-6 scopes.
   Source: ROC, License Classifications, https://roc.az.gov/license-classifications (verified via search summary)
3. **B-5, General Swimming Pool Contractor (residential).** Construct and repair pools and spas, including code-required barriers and utility installation from the service point to the pool equipment; other structures excluded; includes the B-5R subclassifications. **B-6** is the same scope as B-5 plus installation and repair of solar heating devices.
   Source: ROC, License Classifications, https://roc.az.gov/license-classifications (verified via search summary)
4. A **B (General Residential)** contractor must subcontract work on swimming pools and spas (and electrical, plumbing, air conditioning, boilers, water wells) to an appropriately licensed contractor.
   Source: ROC, License Classifications, https://roc.az.gov/license-classifications (verified via search summary)
5. **Cleaning-only work.** No ROC page found that says whether routine pool cleaning or chemical service alone (no repair) needs a license. Do not state either way. Safe wording: "Arizona's Registrar of Contractors licenses residential pool service and repair (R-6) and pool construction (B-5 and B-6). Ask a company which license it holds and look it up."
6. **Minor work ("handyman") exemption, A.R.S. 32-1121(A)(14).** Work on one project with an aggregate contract price, labor and materials included, under **$1,000**, of a casual or minor nature, and the exemption does **not** apply if the work requires a local building permit. Work that is only part of a larger operation does not qualify. ROC guidance: if the whole project costs more than $1,000 or needs a municipal permit, the person or business must be licensed. Bills to raise the limit (SB 1715 in 2023 to $2,000 or $2,500 with inflation indexing; HB 2727 in 2023; HB 2092 in 2024 to $2,500) appeared, but no result showed any enacted; the ROC 2024 Statute and Rules Book still shows $1,000.
   Sources: A.R.S. 32-1121, https://www.azleg.gov/ars/32/01121.htm ; ROC 2024 Statute and Rules Book, https://roc.az.gov/sites/default/files/2024-09/2024%20Statute%20and%20Rules%20Book.pdf ; ROC, "Contracting in Arizona? Here's What You Need to Know!", https://roc.az.gov/news/contracting-arizona-heres-what-you-need-know ; HB2092 summary, https://www.azleg.gov/legtext/56leg/2R/summary/H.HB2092_011224_RA.DOCX.htm (all verified via search summary). Status for October 2026: see "Could NOT verify".
7. **ROC Contractor Search:** https://roc.az.gov/contractor-search . Shows license status ("CURRENT" is the only status legally able to contract; inactive, suspended, expired, revoked, withdrawn and voluntarily canceled cannot), license classification (check that it covers the work in the contract), complaint information (closed cases from the prior two years with complaint ID and outcome), and bond details (bond type, bond company, whether any amount has been paid out on claims).
   Sources: ROC Contractor Search Consumer Guide (PDF), https://roc.az.gov/sites/default/files/2022-07/20201221-Guide.pdf ; ROC, Before You Hire a Contractor, https://roc.az.gov/before-hire ; ROC FAQ, https://roc.az.gov/faq (verified via search summary)
8. **Insurance.** Except for workers' compensation insurance, the ROC does not require an entity to carry insurance to be licensed (workers' comp applies to businesses with employees). Insurance is not shown on the Contractor Search. ROC notes many complaints come from owners who believed they hired a "licensed, bonded and insured" contractor and had not. Copy use: ask a company for proof of liability insurance directly.
   Source: ROC FAQ, https://roc.az.gov/faq ; ROC Before You Hire, https://roc.az.gov/before-hire (verified via search summary)
9. **Bond.** Licensees post a license bond "ranging from $4,250 to $100,000" (ROC Before You Hire / FAQ text; may be dated). Prefer "a license bond, in an amount that depends on the license".
   Source: https://roc.az.gov/before-hire (verified via search summary)
10. **ROC contact:** website https://roc.az.gov ; toll-free **1-877-692-9762** (1-877-MY-AZROC); customer service Monday to Friday, 8:30 am to 4:30 pm.
    Source: ROC, Residential Contractors' Recovery Fund claim form (2023), https://roc.az.gov/sites/default/files/2024-06/RF%20Claim%20Form_2023_fillable.pdf ; ROC Agency page, https://roc.az.gov/agency (verified via search summary)
11. **Residential Contractors' Recovery Fund.** Can pay homeowners harmed by a licensed residential contractor: up to **$30,000 per claimant per residence**, never more than actual damages, no attorney fees (except contested cases appealed to superior court); **$200,000** maximum total per contractor license (pro rata if exceeded). To be eligible, file an ROC complaint that results in suspension or revocation of the contractor's residential or dual license for a violation alleged in the complaint, or obtain a court order for payment from the Fund. Only covers licensed residential contractors.
    Sources: A.R.S. 32-1132.01, https://www.azleg.gov/ars/32/01132-01.htm ; ROC Recovery Fund claim form, https://roc.az.gov/sites/default/files/2024-06/RF%20Claim%20Form_2023_fillable.pdf (verified via search summary)

## 2. Arizona pool enclosure law, A.R.S. 36-1681

12. Applies to a swimming pool or other contained body of water **18 inches or more deep** at any point, **wider than 8 feet** at any point, and intended for swimming; it must be protected by an enclosure surrounding the pool area.
13. Barrier at least **5 feet** high measured on the outside; no opening a 4-inch sphere can pass through; no openings, handholds or footholds on the outside that can be used to climb it; at least **20 inches** from the water's edge.
14. Gates: self-closing and self-latching, open outward away from the pool, latch at least **54 inches** above the ground (or on the pool side with the release at least 5 inches below the top of the gate); a padlock or similar keyed or electric device may be used instead.
15. Where a house wall forms part of the enclosure, the statute sets alternative conditions (ADHS summary mentions a separate barrier at least 4 feet high between the residence and the pool, among other options).
16. Local codes can differ: the statute exempts cities, towns and counties that have their own pool barrier ordinance (equal or more stringent), plus residences where all residents are at least six years old, and pools built before the article took effect. ADHS: the statute was passed to keep children from unsupervised access to residential pools, and applies unless a local code provides otherwise. Copy use: "Check with the City of Yuma or Yuma County building department for the barrier code that applies to your address."
    Sources: A.R.S. 36-1681, https://azleg.gov/ars/36/01681.htm ; Arizona Department of Health Services, Residential Pool Safety Notice, https://www.azdhs.gov/documents/preparedness/epidemiology-disease-control/environmental-health/residential-pool-safety-notice.pdf (verified via search summary). Exemption list carried from 2026-10-20 search summary of the same statute page; not re-shown in today's summary.

## 3. CDC healthy swimming (home pools)

17. **Water levels (current CDC home pool page):** pH **7.0 to 7.8**; free chlorine **at least 1 ppm** in pools; **at least 2 ppm** if cyanuric acid (stabilizer) or stabilized chlorine (dichlor/trichlor) is used; hot tubs at least 3 ppm, and CDC recommends not using cyanuric acid or stabilized chlorine in hot tubs. As pH rises, chlorine's germ-killing power drops, especially above 8.0. Older CDC handouts say pH 7.2 to 7.8; use the current 7.0 to 7.8.
    Source: CDC, Home Pool and Hot Tub Water Treatment and Testing, https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-swimming/about/home-pool-and-hot-tub-water-treatment-and-testing.html (verified via search summary)
18. **Testing frequency:** the search summary attributed "test chlorine and pH at least twice per day, more often when many people use the pool" and "DPD test kit for best accuracy" to the same CDC page. Attribution uncertain (twice daily may be the public-pool guidance). Do not state a frequency from CDC on the site unless re-checked.
19. **Never mix chemicals:** "Never mix chlorine products with acid; this could create toxic gases." "Never mix different pool chemicals (e.g., different types of chlorine products) with each other or with any other substance."
    Source: CDC, Use Pool Chemicals Safely poster, https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-swimming/media/pdfs/Pool-Chemical-Safety-USE-poster-p.pdf ; CDC, Pool Chemical Safety, https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-swimming/toolkit/pool-chemical-safety.html (verified via search summary)
20. **Storage and handling:** use chemicals only in the manufacturer's original, labeled container and never reuse containers; keep chemicals dry; separate incompatible chemicals (for example acid and chlorine); keep them cool in a well-ventilated area away from direct sunlight; store below 95 F (35 C) or as the manufacturer recommends (the 95 F figure is in CDC's aquatic-facility guidance); "ALWAYS secure pool chemicals away from children and animals." CDC's wording is "secure", not "locked"; for home pools CDC says store as the label recommends.
    Sources: CDC storage poster, https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-swimming/media/pdfs/Pool-Chemical-Safety-STORAGE-poster-p.pdf ; CDC Pool Chemical Safety, https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-swimming/toolkit/pool-chemical-safety.html (verified via search summary)
21. **Scale:** CDC reported pool chemical injuries lead to over 4,500 emergency department visits each year (2019 release, 2008 to 2017 data).
    Source: CDC, "Pool Chemical Injuries Lead to Over 4,500 Emergency Department Visits Each Year", https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/media/releases/2019/p0515-pool-chemical-injuries.html ; MMWR, https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/68/wr/mm6819a2.htm (verified via search summary)
22. **Drowning, young children.** CDC Drowning Prevention page: "Drowning is the number one cause of death for children ages 1–4." CDC fact sheet wording: "More children ages 1–4 die from drowning than any other cause of death" (summary said the table layout was garbled; exact wording on the fact sheet should be treated as near-verbatim). "Most drownings happen in home swimming pools among children ages 1–4." Drowning is the second leading cause of unintentional injury death for children 5 to 14, after motor vehicle crashes. Drowning rose 28% among children 1 to 4 in 2022 compared with 2019. (When quoting on the site, replace the en dash with "to": "children ages 1 to 4".)
    Sources: CDC, Drowning Prevention, https://www.cdc.gov/drowning/about/index.html ; CDC, Risk Factors for Drowning, https://www.cdc.gov/drowning/risk-factors/index.html ; CDC Vital Signs, https://www.cdc.gov/vitalsigns/drowning/index.html (verified via search summary)

## 4. CPSC Pool Safely

23. Pool Safely was developed by CPSC to carry out the **Virginia Graeme Baker Pool and Spa Safety Act**, signed into law in December 2007, named for a girl who drowned after a suction entrapment at a spa drain.
24. **Supervision:** a parent or guardian should supervise children in and around pools and spas without texting, calling, reading or other distractions; keep a phone close by; if a child is missing, check the pool first.
25. **Barriers:** CPSC recommends a barrier at least 4 feet high, such as a fence with self-closing, self-latching gates; its guidelines say 5 feet or higher is preferable and some jurisdictions require 60 inches; door alarms on doors leading to the pool where the house forms one side; openings small enough that a 4-inch sphere cannot pass.
26. **Drain covers:** the VGB Act requires compliant drain covers (ASME/ANSI A112.19.8 standard) on public pools and spas; for residential pools CPSC says compliant covers are not required but highly recommended. Never enter a pool or spa with a loose, broken or missing drain cover; it should be closed until repaired.
27. Other steps: learn to swim, learn CPR, keep life rings or reaching poles available.
    Sources: Pool Safely, Safety Tips, https://poolsafely.gov/parents/safety-tips/ ; CPSC, Safety Barrier Guidelines for Residential Pools, https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/362%20Safety%20Barrier%20Guidelines%20for%20Pools.pdf ; Pool Safely, "CPSC Hails Successes of Virginia Graeme Baker Pool & Spa Safety Act", https://www.poolsafely.gov/news/cpsc-hails-successes-of-virginia-graeme-baker-pool-spa-safety-act-as-law-hits-10-year-mark/ ; Pool Safely brochure, https://www.poolsafely.gov/wp-content/uploads/2016/06/PoolSafely_ConsumerEducationalBrochure_ENG.pdf (verified via search summary)
28. **Electrical / GFCI:** CPSC's biggest concern is faulty underwater lighting; older pools are the biggest concern because fixtures may have degraded and may lack GFCI protection. Follow NEC requirements for GFCIs on 120-volt underwater lighting circuits; CPSC recommends GFCIs for circuits at 15 volts or more. Test permanently installed GFCIs monthly (portable ones before each day's use); if a GFCI fails the test, replace it or have an electrician inspect it. CPSC is aware of 33 electrocution deaths in pools and spas since 2002 (figure from an older page; date it or leave off). Site rule: no electrical how-to; refer to a qualified electrician.
    Sources: CPSC, "Don't Swim with Shocks: Electrical Safety In and Around Pools, Spas and Hot Tubs", https://www.cpsc.gov/safety-education/safety-guides/pools-and-spas/dont-swim-shocks-electrical-safety-and-around-pools ; CPSC news, https://www.cpsc.gov/content/dont-swim-with-shocks-cpsc-american-red-cross-warn-of-electrocutions-in-swimming-pools-hot (verified via search summary). Carried from 2026-10-20 (not re-searched): CPSC "What is a GFCI?" says the NEC has required GFCI protection for underwater pool lighting since 1968, https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/099_0.pdf .

## 5. DOE pool pump motor rule and ENERGY STAR

29. **Final rule:** "Energy Conservation Standards for Dedicated Purpose Pool Pump Motors", Federal Register, September 28, 2023. Compliance dates: extra-small motors (under 0.5 total horsepower, THP) and standard-size motors (**1.15 to 5 THP**) manufactured on or after **September 29, 2025**; small-size motors (0.5 to under 1.15 THP) on or after **September 28, 2027**. The rule includes a variable-speed control design requirement for small and standard-size motors.
    Sources: Federal Register, https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2023/09/28/2023-20343/energy-conservation-program-energy-conservation-standards-for-dedicated-purpose-pool-pump-motors ; DOE, Dedicated-Purpose Pool Pump Motors, https://www.energy.gov/cmei/buildings/dedicated-purpose-pool-pump-motors (verified via search summary)
30. **2026 enforcement policy:** DOE policy dated **April 24, 2026** delays enforcement for distribution in commerce of small-size motors manufactured on or after September 28, 2027 but before **March 26, 2029**. No change found to the September 29, 2025 standard-size date.
    Source: DOE Office of the General Counsel, Dedicated Purpose Pool Pump Motors Enforcement Policy, https://www.energy.gov/gc/articles/dedicated-purpose-pool-pump-motors-enforcement-policy (verified via search summary; full policy PDF not seen)
    Safe copy: "Under a U.S. Department of Energy rule, most replacement pool pump motors from 1.15 to 5 total horsepower made on or after September 29, 2025 must have variable-speed control. The rule applies to new motors, not pumps already installed."
31. **ENERGY STAR:** certified in-ground pool pumps can use up to 65% less energy than standard pumps and save up to $450 a year; the extra cost can be paid back in under two years; product finder pages say certified pumps can save over $2,800 over their lifetime. Variable-speed pumps run at the flow each task needs; filtration needs about half the flow of cleaning and about one-eighth the power. Many utilities offer incentives. Older (2014 to 2017) fact sheets gave lower figures; cite the 2020/2021 fact sheet and say "up to".
    Sources: ENERGY STAR, Pool Pumps, https://www.energystar.gov/products/pool_pumps ; ENERGY STAR pool pump fact sheet (2020), https://www.energystar.gov/sites/default/files/tools/ES_PoolPumps_FactSheet_010220_FINAL.pdf ; 2021 fact sheet, https://www.energystar.gov/sites/default/files/tools/ES_PoolPumps_FactSheet_2021_508.pdf ; 2017 fact sheet (flow/power), https://www.energystar.gov/sites/default/files/asset/document/PoolPumps_FactSheet.pdf (verified via search summary)

## 6. Gas safety (pool heaters)

32. If you smell gas (rotten-egg odor, even faint): leave the area or building right away and tell others to leave; do not light anything; do not flip electric switches, thermostats or appliance controls or do anything that could make a spark; do not try to find the leak; once safely away, call 911 and the gas utility; do not go back until emergency officials say it is safe.
    Sources: U.S. DOT PHMSA, Pipeline Leak Recognition and What to Do, https://www.phmsa.dot.gov/safety-awareness/pipeline/pipeline-leak-recognition-and-what-do ; Southwest Gas, Report a Leak, https://www.swgas.com/en/report-a-leak and Home & Business Safety, https://www.swgas.com/en/safety (verified via search summary).
